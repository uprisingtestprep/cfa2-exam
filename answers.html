Vignette CFA2-QUA-001

Priya Nandakumar, a quantitative analyst at Silverwood Capital, is evaluating the drivers of monthly returns for the Meridian Small-Cap Growth Fund. She regresses the fund's monthly excess return (%) on three factors: excess market return (MKT), a size factor (SMB), and a momentum factor (MOM), using 96 months of data. She wants to test whether the assumptions of the classical multiple linear regression model hold before drawing conclusions about factor exposures. Nandakumar reviews the regression output in Exhibit 1. She also plots the model's residuals against fitted values and observes that the spread of residuals widens noticeably as fitted values increase. Separately, she notes the correlation between SMB and MOM is 0.28, and the correlation between MKT and SMB is 0.15. The regression has an R-squared of 0.62 and an F-statistic of 49.8 (with 3 and 92 degrees of freedom), which is highly significant. Nandakumar wants to determine which coefficients are statistically significant at the 5% level, using a critical t-value of approximately 1.99 for 92 degrees of freedom, and to correctly interpret what the widening residual pattern implies for her model.

  1. The correct answer is B (MOM, because its t-statistic of 0.41 is far below the critical value): with a critical t-value of approximately 1.99 for 92 degrees of freedom, only MOM's t-statistic of 0.41 falls short, and its p-value of 0.683 confirms the coefficient is statistically indistinguishable from zero. MKT (t = 8.21) and SMB (t = 2.03) both exceed 1.99, and SMB's p-value of 0.045 is below 0.05, so SMB is significant even though it is close to the threshold.
  2. The correct answer is C (Heteroskedasticity; the standard errors in Exhibit 1 may be unreliable, distorting the t-statistics): a residual spread that widens as fitted values rise is the classic visual signature of heteroskedasticity, where the variance of the error term is not constant across observations. Under the classical linear regression assumptions, OLS coefficient estimates stay unbiased even with heteroskedasticity, but the standard errors computed from the usual formula become invalid, which distorts the t-statistics Nandakumar used to judge significance in question 1. Neither serial correlation nor multicollinearity produces this particular fitted-value-versus-residual fan pattern.
  3. The correct answer is A (Reject the null hypothesis that all slope coefficients equal zero; the factors jointly explain significant variance): the reported F-statistic of 49.8 far exceeds the critical F-value of approximately 2.70 at the 5% level with 3 and 92 degrees of freedom, so Nandakumar rejects the null that all three slope coefficients are jointly zero. An R-squared of 0.62, while below 0.70, has no bearing on the F-test's conclusion since the test evaluates joint significance, not goodness-of-fit thresholds. The joint F-test does not attribute significance to any single variable, so option C's claim that only MKT drives the result misapplies the test.
  4. The correct answer is B (1.15 percentage points, matching the estimated coefficient on MKT directly): in a multiple regression, each slope coefficient measures the change in the dependent variable for a one-unit change in that independent variable while holding the other independent variables constant. Exhibit 1 reports the MKT coefficient as 1.15, so holding SMB and MOM fixed, a 1 percentage point increase in excess market return is associated with a 1.15 percentage point increase in the fund's expected excess return. The 0.42 and 0.09 values in options A and C belong to the intercept and MOM respectively, not to MKT.
Vignette CFA2-ECO-011

Lena Voss, a currency analyst at Nordholm Capital, is evaluating whether a one-year forward contract quoted by Dorevan Bank between the Krysenia krona (KRZ) and the Talvora lira (TAL) offers a riskless arbitrage opportunity. The current spot exchange rate is 4.5000 TAL per KRZ. The one-year risk-free interest rate is 3.00% in Talvora and 6.00% in Krysenia. Dorevan Bank is quoting a one-year forward rate of 4.4200 TAL per KRZ, which Voss compares against the rate implied by covered interest rate parity. Voss's exhibit summarizes the spot rate, both money market interest rates, and the bank's quoted forward rate. She plans to determine the no-arbitrage forward rate, identify whether the quoted forward rate creates a profitable arbitrage strategy, and explain which currency should trade at a forward discount given the interest rate differential between the two economies. She also wants to understand how a change in Talvora's interest rate would affect the theoretical forward rate, holding the Krysenia rate and the spot rate constant, before advising Nordholm's trading desk on whether to act on Dorevan Bank's quoted forward rate.

  1. The correct answer is A (4.3726, equal to the spot rate multiplied by the ratio of the two interest rates.): covered interest rate parity states that the forward rate equals the spot rate multiplied by (1 plus the domestic rate) divided by (1 plus the foreign rate). Using the exhibit's data, the forward rate equals 4.5000 times 1.03 divided by 1.06, which equals approximately 4.3726 TAL per KRZ. This is the rate that eliminates any riskless arbitrage between the two money markets and the spot exchange market. Any quoted forward rate that differs from 4.3726, such as Dorevan Bank's 4.4200, signals a mispricing relative to parity.
  2. The correct answer is B (Borrow Talvora lira, convert to Krysenia krona at spot, invest at 6.00%, and sell krona forward at 4.4200.): because Dorevan Bank's quoted forward of 4.4200 exceeds the parity forward of 4.3726, krona sold forward are worth more Talvora lira than parity justifies. Borrowing TAL at 3.00%, converting to KRZ at spot, investing the KRZ at 6.00%, and locking in the sale of the KRZ proceeds forward at 4.4200 generates enough TAL to repay the loan with a riskless surplus left over. The other two strategies either reverse the correct direction or leave the position uncovered, exposing it to currency risk rather than locking in the arbitrage profit.
  3. The correct answer is B (Krysenia krona, because it carries the higher one-year money market interest rate of the two currencies.): under covered interest rate parity, the currency with the higher interest rate must trade at a forward discount so that no arbitrage opportunity exists. Since Krysenia's 6.00% rate exceeds Talvora's 3.00% rate, the krona's forward rate (4.3726) sits below the spot rate (4.5000) when expressed as TAL per KRZ, meaning fewer lira are received per krona forward than at spot. This discount exactly offsets the krona's higher money market return, not merely the bank's quoting convention.
  4. The correct answer is C (Rise above 4.3726, since a smaller interest rate differential narrows the krona's forward discount.): recomputing the parity forward with Talvora's rate at 5.00% gives 4.5000 times 1.05 divided by 1.06, which equals approximately 4.4575, above the original 4.3726. Because the interest rate differential between Talvora and Krysenia shrinks from 3 percentage points to 1 percentage point, the krona's required forward discount narrows correspondingly, pushing the theoretical forward rate closer to the unchanged spot rate of 4.5000 rather than further away from it, as option A suggests.
Vignette CFA2-ECO-017

Tobias Marchetti, an economist at Ferrowind Research, is testing whether the one-year forward exchange rate has behaved as an unbiased predictor of the future spot exchange rate between the Drevalian crown (DRC) and the Lucanor franc (LUF) over the past five years. For each year, Marchetti records the one-year forward rate quoted at the start of the year and the realized spot rate observed one year later, both expressed as Drevalian crowns per Lucanor franc, along with the resulting forecast error. His exhibit summarizes this five-year history. Marchetti wants to compute the average forecast error across the five years, interpret what a consistently positive average forecast error implies about whether the forward rate underpredicted or overpredicted the realized spot rate, state what relationship would hold between the forward rate and the expected future spot rate if the unbiased forward rate hypothesis held exactly, and explain what phenomenon is consistent with the hypothesis failing empirically in the way his data shows.

  1. The correct answer is A (Positive 0.16, indicating the realized spot rate exceeded the forward rate on average across the years.): averaging the five forecast errors in the exhibit, 0.25 minus 0.10 plus 0.40 minus 0.10 plus 0.35, gives a sum of 0.80, and dividing by five years gives an average forecast error of positive 0.16. This is not negative, and it uses all five years rather than only the two largest positive errors, so the average correctly reflects a consistent positive bias across the full sample.
  2. The correct answer is C (Systematically underpredicted the realized future spot rate for the DRC per LUF exchange rate.): a positive forecast error is defined as the realized spot rate minus the forward rate, so a consistently positive average error of 0.16 means the realized spot rate came in higher than the forward rate had predicted, year after year. This means the forward rate was too low relative to what actually occurred, which is the definition of systematic underprediction, not overprediction or unbiasedness.
  3. The correct answer is A (The forward rate equals the expected future spot rate, with forecast errors averaging zero over time.): the unbiased forward rate hypothesis states that the forward rate is the market's best unbiased estimate of the future spot rate, meaning forecast errors should average to zero over a long enough sample rather than showing a persistent positive or negative bias. It makes no claim that the forward rate is unrelated to expectations, nor that it is simply the spot rate adjusted for inflation.
  4. The correct answer is B (A time-varying currency risk premium that drives a wedge between the forward rate and the expected spot rate.): a persistent, non-zero average forecast error across five years is difficult to reconcile with pure unbiasedness, and the standard explanation in the currency literature is that a time-varying risk premium separates the forward rate from the market's true expected future spot rate, rather than the forward rate purely reflecting that expectation. This is a more direct explanation than assuming a data error or a fixed exchange rate regime, which the data itself contradicts.
Vignette CFA2-COR-021

Priya Nandan, a credit analyst, is evaluating the capital structure and cost of capital of Veltrix Industrial Corp., a mid-cap manufacturer of precision bearings. Veltrix's market value of debt is $400 million and its market value of equity is $600 million, giving a total capitalization of $1,000 million. The company's outstanding bonds carry a pre-tax yield to maturity of 6.0%, and Veltrix's marginal tax rate is 25%. Nandan estimates the cost of equity using the capital asset pricing model (CAPM), applying a risk-free rate of 3.5%, an equity risk premium of 5.5%, and Veltrix's estimated equity beta of 1.2. Nandan summarizes her inputs in Exhibit 1 before computing Veltrix's weighted average cost of capital (WACC) for use in an upcoming capital budgeting analysis. She notes that Veltrix's finance department is also considering a debt issuance and an equity issuance separately, and she wants to understand how each financing choice would affect the firm's cost of capital inputs going forward.

  1. The correct answer is A (7.9%, combining a 40% debt weight at 4.5% after-tax cost with equity at 10.1%): Using CAPM, cost of equity = 3.5% + 1.2 × 5.5% = 10.1%. After-tax cost of debt = 6.0% × (1 minus 0.25) = 4.5%. WACC = (0.40 × 4.5%) + (0.60 × 10.1%) = 1.8% + 6.06% = 7.86%, closest to 7.9%. Options using the pre-tax cost of debt or misapplying the equity weight to the cost of debt produce a materially different, incorrect figure.
  2. The correct answer is C (decrease to 4.2%, since a higher tax rate lowers the after-tax cost of debt): After-tax cost of debt equals the pre-tax yield multiplied by one minus the tax rate. At a 30% tax rate, 6.0% × (1 minus 0.30) = 4.2%, down from the original 4.5%. A higher tax rate increases the value of the interest tax shield, which lowers the after-tax cost of debt and, holding weights constant, reduces WACC. The bond's coupon rate itself is not what drives this calculation.
  3. The correct answer is B (34.8%, since total capitalization rises to $1,150 million after the offering): New total capitalization = $400 million debt + $750 million equity = $1,150 million. New debt weight = $400 million divided by $1,150 million = 34.8%. Because Veltrix's cost of capital weights are based on market values, issuing new equity mechanically lowers the debt weight even though the dollar amount of debt outstanding has not changed.
  4. The correct answer is C (11.8%, reflecting the higher systematic risk premium at the new beta): Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium = 3.5% + (1.5 × 5.5%) = 3.5% + 8.25% = 11.75%, closest to 11.8%. A higher beta increases the equity risk premium component proportionally, raising the CAPM-derived cost of equity and, all else equal, increasing WACC.
Vignette CFA2-COR-027

Aiden Brustkern, an analyst at Corentin Group, is evaluating the cost of equity for a proposed manufacturing expansion into the Republic of Tavara, an emerging market. Brustkern uses the sovereign yield spread approach to estimate Tavara's country risk premium: Tavara's dollar-denominated sovereign bonds yield 3.0% more than comparable U.S. Treasuries, Tavara's equity index has an annualized standard deviation of 30%, and Tavara's dollar-denominated sovereign bond has an annualized standard deviation of 12%. Brustkern applies a mature-market equity risk premium of 5.0%, a U.S. risk-free rate of 3.0%, and estimates the project's equity beta, relative to the Tavara market, at 1.1. He summarizes these inputs in Exhibit 1 and plans to compare the resulting cost of equity for the Tavara project against an otherwise identical domestic project that uses only the mature-market equity risk premium.

  1. The correct answer is A (7.5%, the sovereign yield spread multiplied by the ratio of equity to bond volatility): The sovereign yield spread approach estimates the country risk premium as the sovereign bond yield spread multiplied by the annualized standard deviation of the equity market divided by the annualized standard deviation of the sovereign bond. Here, 3.0% × (30% divided by 12%) = 3.0% × 2.5 = 7.5%.
  2. The correct answer is C (12.5%, the sum of the mature-market premium and the country risk premium): The total equity risk premium for a project in an emerging market equals the mature-market equity risk premium plus the country risk premium: 5.0% + 7.5% = 12.5%. This combined premium reflects both the baseline compensation investors require for holding equities and the additional compensation required for Tavara-specific risk.
  3. The correct answer is B (16.75%, combining the total equity risk premium, project beta, and risk-free rate): Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × total equity risk premium = 3.0% + (1.1 × 12.5%) = 3.0% + 13.75% = 16.75%. This is materially higher than a comparable domestic project because the 12.5% premium already embeds Tavara's country risk.
  4. The correct answer is B (8.25 percentage points, reflecting the country risk premium scaled by beta): The domestic project's cost of equity = 3.0% + (1.1 × 5.0%) = 8.5%. The Tavara project's cost of equity = 16.75%. The difference, 16.75% minus 8.5% = 8.25 percentage points, equals the country risk premium of 7.5% scaled by the project beta of 1.1 (7.5% × 1.1 = 8.25%), not the raw, unscaled country risk premium itself.
Vignette CFA2-DER-031

Elena Cho, a portfolio manager at Marrow Capital, wants to enter into a 9-month forward contract on the common stock of NorthGate Retail Inc. The stock currently trades at $80.00 per share. NorthGate is expected to pay two dividends during the life of the contract: $1.00 per share in 3 months and $1.00 per share in 6 months. The annualized risk-free rate is 4%, quoted on a simple-interest basis. Cho wants to determine the no-arbitrage forward price that should apply today, and later wants to know the value of her forward position after four months have passed, at which point NorthGate shares trade at $83.00, the first dividend has already been paid, and only the second $1.00 dividend, due in two more months, remains outstanding.

  1. The correct answer is A ($1.97): each dividend is discounted to today at the 4% annualized risk-free rate for the time until it is paid. The 3-month dividend has present value 1.00 ÷ (1 + 0.04 × 3÷12) = 1.00 ÷ 1.01 = $0.9901, and the 6-month dividend has present value 1.00 ÷ (1 + 0.04 × 6÷12) = 1.00 ÷ 1.02 = $0.9804. Summing these gives $0.9901 + $0.9804 = $1.9705, closest to $1.97.
  2. The correct answer is C ($80.37): the no-arbitrage forward price equals the stock price minus the present value of dividends, then compounded forward at the risk-free rate for the full contract term. Subtracting the $1.9705 present value of dividends from the $80.00 spot price gives $78.0295, and compounding this at 4% for 9 months gives $78.0295 × (1 + 0.04 × 9÷12) = $78.0295 × 1.03 = $80.37. This reflects the cost of carry net of the dividends the stock pays before maturity.
  3. The correct answer is A ($2.95): the value of a long forward equals the current spot price minus the present value of remaining dividends, minus the present value of the original forward price discounted over the remaining time to maturity. The remaining $1.00 dividend (due in 2 months) has present value 1.00 ÷ (1 + 0.04 × 2÷12) = $0.9934, so $83.00 minus $0.9934 equals $82.0066. Discounting the original $80.37 forward price over the remaining 5 months gives $80.37 ÷ (1 + 0.04 × 5÷12) = $79.05, and $82.0066 minus $79.05 equals approximately $2.95.
  4. The correct answer is B (The value to the long is zero at initiation because the forward price is set to eliminate arbitrage opportunities): the no-arbitrage forward price is deliberately chosen so that neither party pays or receives anything to enter the contract, meaning its initial value is zero to both the long and the short. Only as the underlying stock price and remaining time to maturity change does the contract acquire positive or negative value to either side. This is a defining feature that distinguishes a forward's initiation value from its value at any later date.
Vignette CFA2-DER-037

Tobias Lindqvist, a risk manager, monitors a firm proprietary position that is long 500 identical European call options on Dunmore Robotics Inc. common stock. Each option currently has a delta of 0.62, a gamma of 0.04, and a vega of 0.18. Dunmore shares currently trade at $120.00 and are expected to rise to $121.50 over the next trading day, a $1.50 increase, with no material change expected in implied volatility over that horizon. Lindqvist wants to estimate the resulting dollar change in the value of a single option and of the entire 500-option position using the Greeks provided, and to understand how an unexpected rise in implied volatility alone would affect the position's value.

  1. The correct answer is A ($0.93): using delta alone, the estimated dollar change in an option's value for a small change in the stock price equals delta multiplied by the change in stock price, or 0.62 × $1.50 = $0.93. Delta measures the option's sensitivity to small movements in the underlying stock price and is the first-order, linear approximation of that sensitivity. This approximation becomes less accurate as the size of the price move increases, which is exactly why gamma is used to refine the estimate.
  2. The correct answer is B ($0.98): adding a gamma adjustment refines the delta-only estimate using the formula (delta × price change) + (0.5 × gamma × price change²), or (0.62 × $1.50) + (0.5 × 0.04 × $1.50²) = $0.93 + $0.045 = $0.975, which rounds to $0.98. Gamma captures the curvature in the option's value as the stock price moves, correcting for the fact that delta itself changes as the stock price changes. Including gamma produces a more accurate estimate than delta alone, particularly for larger price movements.
  3. The correct answer is C ($465): the position-level dollar change equals the per-option delta-based estimate multiplied by the number of options held, or $0.93 × 500 = $465. This scales the single-option estimate calculated using delta alone up to the full 500-option position that Lindqvist is monitoring. A more precise estimate could also incorporate gamma at the position level, which would produce a total change closer to $0.975 × 500 = $487.50.
  4. The correct answer is A (Increase, because vega is positive for a long call option position): vega measures the sensitivity of an option's value to changes in implied volatility, and both long calls and long puts have positive vega, meaning their values rise when implied volatility increases, holding the stock price constant. Higher volatility increases the chance of a large favorable stock price move before expiration, which raises the expected payoff of the option without increasing the fixed downside risk of the premium already paid. This is why option buyers, whether holding calls or puts, generally benefit from an unexpected rise in implied volatility.
Vignette CFA2-ALT-041

Sofia Reyes, CFA, is a real estate analyst valuing Meridian Point, a stabilized office tower, for a pension fund client considering an acquisition. The property is fully leased to investment-grade tenants under long-term leases with predictable rent escalations, and Reyes believes the income approach is the most reliable valuation method given the property's income-producing nature and the limited availability of closely comparable recent sales. She assembles Year 1 operating projections shown in Exhibit 1, and she obtains a market-derived direct capitalization rate of 6.4% from recently sold comparable office assets with similar tenant credit quality and lease terms. Reyes plans to use direct capitalization to estimate value and to test how sensitive that estimate is to changes in the market cap rate.

  1. The correct answer is A ($1,424,000, reflecting effective gross income of $2,208,000 less operating expenses of $784,000): Exhibit 1 shows potential gross income of $2,400,000 reduced by an 8% vacancy and collection loss of $192,000 to arrive at effective gross income of $2,208,000. Net operating income equals effective gross income less operating expenses, so $2,208,000 minus $784,000 equals $1,424,000. This net operating income figure is the base Reyes will use for direct capitalization, since it reflects income after both vacancy and operating costs are removed.
  2. The correct answer is C ($22,250,000, dividing net operating income of $1,424,000 by the 6.4% cap rate identified from comparable office sales): Under direct capitalization, value equals net operating income divided by the market capitalization rate. Dividing Meridian Point's $1,424,000 net operating income by the 6.4% cap rate gives $1,424,000 divided by 0.064, which equals $22,250,000. This approach directly capitalizes a single year of stabilized income into an indicated value, consistent with the property's stable, long-term lease structure.
  3. The correct answer is B (stable contractual income and reliable market cap rate data make the income approach most reliable here): Meridian Point's fully leased, long-term contracts to investment-grade tenants produce predictable, contractually based income streams, and Reyes has obtained a credible market-derived cap rate from truly comparable office sales. These two facts together make direct capitalization of net operating income the most defensible valuation method for this specific property, rather than the approach being inherently superior in every situation. The limited availability of closely comparable sales further reduces the reliability of the sales comparison approach in this case.
  4. The correct answer is A ($20,640,000, since a higher cap rate lowers value for unchanged net operating income): Dividing the unchanged $1,424,000 net operating income by the higher 6.9% cap rate gives $1,424,000 divided by 0.069, which equals approximately $20,637,681, rounding to $20,640,000 at the nearest $10,000. This illustrates the inverse relationship between capitalization rates and value under direct capitalization: holding net operating income constant, a higher required cap rate always produces a lower indicated value. The roughly $1.6 million decline from the original $22,250,000 estimate reflects only the 50 basis point increase in the discount applied to the same income stream.
Vignette CFA2-FIN-051

On January 1, Year 1, Castellan Corp acquired a 30% interest in Nordholt Supply Co for $60 million cash, obtaining significant influence but not control, and elected to account for the investment using the equity method. At the acquisition date, Nordholt's identifiable net assets had a book value of $150 million, equal to fair value except for a piece of specialized equipment, which was undervalued on Nordholt's books by $9 million and had a remaining useful life of 6 years. The remainder of the $15 million excess of the purchase price over Castellan's 30% share of book value was attributed to goodwill, which is not amortized. During Year 1, Nordholt reported net income of $20 million and paid cash dividends of $5 million to its shareholders. Castellan's controller, Andrea Voss, is preparing the equity method entries needed to reflect Castellan's 30% interest in Nordholt for the Year 1 financial statements, including amortization of the excess purchase price allocated to the undervalued equipment and the treatment of dividends received as a return of the investment rather than as investment income.

  1. The correct answer is A ($4.5 million in equity income from the investee for Year 1): Castellan's 30% share of Nordholt's $20 million net income is $6.0 million, but the $9 million excess purchase price allocated to the undervalued equipment must be amortized over its 6-year remaining life, adding $1.5 million of annual amortization that reduces equity income. Net equity income equals $6.0 million minus $1.5 million, or $4.5 million. The $6 million allocated to goodwill is not amortized, so it does not further reduce equity income.
  2. The correct answer is A ($63.0 million ending investment account balance under the equity method): the investment account begins at $60.0 million, increases by $4.5 million of equity income (30% of Nordholt's net income less $1.5 million of excess purchase price amortization), and decreases by $1.5 million for Castellan's 30% share of the $5 million dividend, which is treated as a return of investment. The ending balance is $60.0 million plus $4.5 million minus $1.5 million, or $63.0 million.
  3. The correct answer is C ($6 million of the excess purchase price allocated to goodwill): the total excess of the $60 million purchase price over Castellan's 30% share of Nordholt's $150 million book value is $15 million. Of that excess, $9 million is specifically attributable to the undervalued equipment identified at acquisition, so the remaining $6 million, which cannot be attributed to any specific identifiable asset, is allocated to goodwill. Goodwill recognized under the equity method is not separately amortized.
  4. The correct answer is C (decreases the investment account balance by $1.5 million, the proportionate dividend share): under the equity method, dividends received from an investee are treated as a return of the original investment rather than as income, because the investor already recognized its share of the investee's earnings through equity income. Castellan's 30% share of Nordholt's $5 million dividend is $1.5 million, which reduces the investment account by that amount. The dividend has no effect on Castellan's income statement.
Vignette CFA2-FIN-057

Umbra Networks reports comparative pension footnote data for Year 1 and Year 2, which analyst Priya Osei is reviewing to assess whether Umbra's reported operating margin overstates the economic cost of its pension plan. In Year 1, service cost was $14 million, interest cost was $22 million, expected return on plan assets was $26 million, and $8 million of actuarial losses were recognized in other comprehensive income, producing net periodic pension cost of $10 million. In Year 2, service cost rose to $16 million, interest cost rose to $24 million, expected return on plan assets rose to $28 million, and $5 million of actuarial gains were recognized in other comprehensive income, producing net periodic pension cost of $12 million; the discount rate used to measure the obligation declined from 5.5% in Year 1 to 5.0% in Year 2. Priya must determine how the non-service components of pension cost are typically presented on the income statement, how the decline in the discount rate would be expected to affect the pension benefit obligation, what net periodic pension cost of $12 million in Year 2 does and does not include, and the longer-run effect of consistently overstating expected returns relative to actual returns.

  1. The correct answer is B (outside operating income, often in a separate non-operating income statement line): under current US GAAP presentation requirements, only the service cost component of net periodic pension cost is included within operating income alongside other employee compensation costs. The remaining components, including interest cost, expected return on plan assets, and amortization of actuarial gains and losses, are presented outside operating income, often within a separate non-operating line on the income statement.
  2. The correct answer is A (Increase the pension benefit obligation): a lower discount rate increases the present value of the future benefit payments that make up the pension benefit obligation, since those future cash flows are discounted at a smaller rate. The obligation does not decrease, since a lower discount rate raises rather than lowers a present value calculation, and the direction of the effect is fully determinable from the discount-rate change alone: a lower rate always increases the present value of a fixed set of future payments.
  3. The correct answer is B (amortization of the actuarial gain recognized in other comprehensive income): Umbra's Year 2 net periodic pension cost of $12 million reflects only service cost, interest cost, and expected return on plan assets, which together equal $16 million plus $24 million minus $28 million, or $12 million. It does not include any amortization of the $5 million actuarial gain recognized separately in other comprehensive income during Year 2, since that gain has not been reclassified into net income.
  4. The correct answer is A (net periodic pension cost is understated, overstating reported operating performance): expected return on plan assets is subtracted when calculating net periodic pension cost, so a systematically inflated expected return assumption relative to actual returns will understate the true economic cost of the pension plan each year. This understated cost flows through to net income, making Umbra's reported operating performance appear stronger than it would under a more realistic return assumption.
Vignette CFA2-EQU-068

Priya Nandakumar, CFA, covers Bellhaven Corp, a stable regional utility holding company. Bellhaven just paid an annual dividend of $2.40 per share and has maintained a constant historical growth rate that Nandakumar expects to continue indefinitely at 4.0% per year. Given Bellhaven's low business risk and stable regulatory environment, Nandakumar estimates a required return on equity of 9.0%. Bellhaven common shares currently trade at $46.00 on the exchange. Nandakumar plans to use the Gordon growth model to determine whether the stock is fairly priced, recognizing that the model requires the required rate of return to exceed the assumed perpetual growth rate for a finite, positive value to result. She also notes that Bellhaven's payout policy has been stable for over a decade, supporting the constant-growth assumption underlying her valuation.

  1. The correct answer is B ($2.50 per share, reflecting a 4.0% increase applied to the current dividend): D1 equals D0 × (1+g) = $2.40 × 1.04 = $2.496, which rounds to approximately $2.50. Option A incorrectly uses the current dividend with no growth applied. Option C confuses the required return with the dividend growth rate.
  2. The correct answer is B ($49.92, calculated by dividing next year's expected dividend by the spread between return and growth): V0 = D1 ÷ (r − g) = $2.496 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = $2.496 ÷ 0.05 = $49.92. Option A ignores growth in the denominator, understating value. Option C would require an invalid model where growth exceeds the required return.
  3. The correct answer is A (undervalued, since the calculated intrinsic value exceeds the current market trading price): the model's intrinsic value of $49.92 is above the $46.00 market price, indicating Bellhaven shares are trading below their estimated fair value. First, when he plots residuals against time, he sees clear positive autocorrelation: periods of positive residuals tend to be followed by more positive residuals. Second, he calculates the correlation between leverage ratio and interest coverage ratio at negative 0.81, and a regression of leverage ratio on the other two independent variables produces an R-squared of 0.88. Delacroix runs a Durbin-Watson test and obtains a statistic of 1.04, well below the expected value of 2 under no autocorrelation. He is unsure whether to use robust standard errors, first-difference the data, or drop a variable, and wants to understand each problem's real consequence before choosing a remedy.

  1. The correct answer is C (Positive serial correlation; standard errors are understated, inflating the reported t-statistics): a Durbin-Watson statistic of 1.04, well below the benchmark value of 2, indicates positive serial correlation in the residuals, consistent with Delacroix's plot showing runs of same-signed residuals over time. Positive serial correlation causes the usual OLS standard error formula to understate the true variability of the coefficient estimates, which mechanically inflates the t-statistics and can make variables look more statistically significant than they really are. This condition does not bias the coefficient point estimates themselves, only the precision attributed to them.
  2. The correct answer is B (Multicollinearity; individual coefficient standard errors become inflated even though overall model fit stays high): a strong negative correlation of 0.81 between leverage ratio and interest coverage ratio, combined with an R-squared of 0.88 when leverage is regressed on the other independent variables, is direct evidence that leverage ratio is highly collinear with the remaining regressors. Multicollinearity leaves the overall regression fit and F-statistic largely intact, since the combined explanatory power of the correlated variables is still captured, but it inflates the standard errors on the individual affected coefficients. This makes it hard to isolate the separate contribution of leverage ratio versus interest coverage ratio even though the model as a whole may explain bond spreads well.
  3. The correct answer is A (Compute serial-correlation-consistent standard errors, such as those produced by the Hansen method): serial-correlation-consistent, or heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent, standard errors directly correct the understated standard errors caused by positive serial correlation without changing the coefficient estimates or requiring any variable to be dropped. Dropping the leverage ratio variable would address multicollinearity, not the Durbin-Watson finding of 1.04, and would sacrifice information from a theoretically relevant variable. Adding an interaction term does not target serial correlation in the error term at all.
  4. The correct answer is C (Failing to reject the null hypothesis for one or both coefficients even though the variable truly matters): because multicollinearity inflates the standard errors of the correlated coefficients, the resulting t-statistics shrink even when the underlying variables genuinely explain bond spreads, raising the risk of a Type II error, a false failure to reject the null hypothesis of no effect. The overall regression's F-statistic and R-squared are largely unaffected by multicollinearity since the joint explanatory power of the correlated variables remains, so options A and B describe outcomes multicollinearity does not typically cause. Interpreting individual t-statistics at face value in this setting risks discarding a truly important variable as statistically insignificant.

Vignette CFA2-ECO-016

Haruto Vinsen, an economist at Coastwise Research, compares two economies, Palmerio and Duskrath, to evaluate whether the neoclassical convergence hypothesis explains their relative growth. Palmerio is a low-income economy with a capital-to-labor ratio well below its long-run steady state, an open capital account, strong protection of property rights, and a household savings rate of 22% of GDP. Duskrath is a high-income economy already near its own steady-state capital-to-labor ratio, with a savings rate of 18% of GDP, and both economies have broadly similar access to production technology. Vinsen also examines a third economy, Feralnos, which shares a comparable initial income level with Palmerio but has weak property rights protection and a closed capital account, and has shown no meaningful tendency to catch up to Duskrath's income level over three full decades. Vinsen wants to determine whether the pattern across all three economies is more consistent with absolute convergence or with club convergence, and what would happen to Palmerio's catch-up path if its savings rate fell to match Duskrath's own savings rate.

  1. The correct answer is A (The neoclassical convergence hypothesis, under which a poorer economy with similar structural features catches up.): Palmerio has a capital-to-labor ratio well below its steady state along with an open capital account and strong property rights, the exact conditions under which the neoclassical convergence hypothesis predicts faster growth and gradual catch-up toward a structurally similar, wealthier economy like Duskrath. Club convergence would instead require the two economies to share similar structural characteristics as a group, and the vignette gives no basis for assuming a closed-capital-account requirement, since Palmerio's capital account is explicitly open.
  2. The correct answer is C (Weak property rights and closed capital account, which prevent the structural conditions convergence requires.): the neoclassical convergence hypothesis assumes an economy can attract or deploy capital efficiently and that returns to investment are secure, conditions Feralnos lacks due to its closed capital account and weak property rights, unlike Palmerio. This structural gap, not its capital-to-labor ratio relative to Duskrath or its initial income level, best explains why Feralnos has failed to catch up despite starting from a comparably low income level.
  3. The correct answer is B (Club convergence, in which only economies sharing similar structural characteristics converge toward one another.): Palmerio, which shares Duskrath's open capital markets, strong institutions, and technology access, is expected to converge, while Feralnos, which starts from a similar income level but lacks those structural features, is not. This selective pattern, convergence only within the subset of economies sharing similar fundamentals, is precisely what the club convergence hypothesis describes, rather than a universal absolute convergence or outright divergence regardless of structural similarity.
  4. The correct answer is C (Fall, slowing the pace at which Palmerio catches up toward Duskrath's higher income level.): in the basic neoclassical growth framework, a lower savings rate reduces the steady-state capital-to-labor ratio an economy converges toward, since less output is reinvested as capital. A savings rate cut from 22% to 18% would therefore lower Palmerio's target steady state rather than raise it, which slows, rather than accelerates, its projected catch-up growth toward Duskrath.
